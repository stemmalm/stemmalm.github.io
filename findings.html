Findings

What the individuals have shown so far.

Each individual hears only what adults said to children, grows up month by month, later reads children’s books, and can teach the next generation by copying what it heard. At every stage the question is the same: which properties of language grow out of this input alone, and which need something the input does not contain.

1 · 0–72 months

It speaks like an adult before it speaks like a child.

#1–#3children in the same recordings

At 12 months #1–#3 put 3.5 real words into an utterance; children in the same recordings put 0.9. The short, telegraphic speech of a two-year-old does not grow out of adult input alone.

2 · 0–72 months

Word endings come early. Agreement stays shaky.

regular pastsubject–verb agreementirregular pastchance

Regular past tense passes at 12–15 months. Subject–verb agreement hovers near 70% for six years. Irregular past drops below chance at 8–12 months and then recovers: over-regularisation without the earlier phase of correct, memorised forms that children show. On a wider battery of 23 grammar tests, two or three are learnt after the first months.

3 · 0–72 months

A bigger learner, or more of the right sentences, did not teach more grammar.

A learner 2.4 times larger passed as many tests as the original, and memorised its input instead: lower training loss, worse on held-out speech. Doubling the sentences with determiners, negative polarity items or irregular verbs did no better than doubling random sentences. Both are weak tests: for two of the three phenomena the added sentences did not match what the test asks.

4 · 72–144 months

Books alone erase conversation. Three parts in ten of talk keep it.

books only (#1–#3)books + 30% talk (#10–#12)

After six months of books only, held-out adult speech was as hard for #1–#3 as at a much younger age, and it kept getting worse. Clones of the same individuals heard the same books with 30% conversation mixed in: conversation held, and the books were learnt just as well. Question agreement went back to chance in the books-only group; irregular past, common in storybooks, got better.

5 · next generation

Children inherit the slips of the copy they heard.

Grown #1 and #2 each copied the adult input, slipping on 5% of words and writing their own word instead. Children raised on #1’s copy (#4–#6) and on #2’s copy (#7–#9) each said more of their own parent’s new word pairs. Cross ratio 2.51 (95% interval 1.70–3.80, permutation p = 0.0005). The more often a slip appeared in the copy, the more the children used it.
